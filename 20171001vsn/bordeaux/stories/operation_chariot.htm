ESCOUBLAC-LA BAULE WAR CEMETERY and Operation Chariot

The Escoublac-La Baule War Cemetery used to be situated just outside La Baule. With the growth of the town it is now surrounded by houses. It is situated about 18Km from St Nazaire which is where practically all those buried there were killed.

Although 430 KM North West of Bordeaux and therefore hardly in our geographical area, I felt that the importance of some of the WW2 events that took place there should not be left untold. 331 men are buried in this cemetery ; 192 from the Army, 91 from the Air Force , 42 from the Navy , and 3 from the Merchant Navy ; they are either British, Canadian, Australian, New Zealander, or Polish . As in the 1914 - 1918 War St Nazaire was one of the ports used by the British Expeditionary Force, and later the Americans to land their troops and equipment and form up the units before they joined the front.

This meant that in June 1940, after the British troops in the Calais and Boulogne areas had been evacuated from the beaches near Dunkerque, there remained those in the St Nazaire area to be evacuated. These were either the troops stationed there or some of the front line troops who had succeeded in rejoining that area. On the 14th June it was decided to evacuate them all. This was successfully carried out by the 25th June, except for the disastrous sinking of the Lancastria in the Loire estuary by the German Air Force with the loss of over 3000.

A few of those killed lie in this cemetery but many were washed ashore all along the Atlantic coast and are buried all the way down to the Gironde estuary. Most of the Air Force crews buried there were part of Bomber Command and were shot down in that area either by the German Anti Aircraft Batteries or the Luftwaffe fighters.

Early in the war the Germans decided to build some several submarine pens in St Nazaire which would thus become an important base for their Atlantic war against the Allied convoys. The construction of this formidable concrete construction on a silt soil proved to be very difficult and had to be renewed after several failures due to storms. It is unfortunate that the deciders were not able or not willing to effect the required quantity of bombing raids to the site during this period in which it was most vulnerable. When this was done in 1942 it was too late 9 of the 14 pens were completed and totally invulnerable. These raids, carried out by high flying US Air Force Flying Fortresses, were of practically no effect on the submarine pens but, because these were very close to the town centre, destroyed over 60% of St Nazaire, peaking on the 28th February 1943. The surviving population had no option but to move out into the adjacent villages.

Another asset of St Nazaire as far as the Germans were concerned was its capacity to take their largest warships into dry dock. Specialised before the war in the construction of the largest liners such as Normandie or battleships like the Jean Bart it was the only Atlantic port capable of repairing their Scharnhost, or Gneisnau or Tirpitz warships which they desperately also wanted to use against the Allied convoys in the Atlantic. This is what decided Admiral Mountbatten to organise a Combined Operation Raid, code name Operation Chariot, on St Nazaire to put the dry dock known as the Forme Joubert out of action for the rest of the war. This was to be done by ramming and blowing up the dock gates, and destroying its pumping system. The date chosen was the night of the 27/28th March 1942.

An old US lend-lease warship renamed Campbeltown was loaded with 4.5 tons of explosives, modified in its structures to resemble a Möge type German warship. It flew German colours until it reached the proximity of the Loire estuary. The Campbeltown was accompanied by 17 motor launches carrying the Commando units and intended also for the return of the survivors. Despite very heavy defensive fire from the strong German batteries, Commander Rider drove the Campbeltown straight into the southern gates of the entrance lock to the dry dock. Meanwhile the Captain Newman's Commandos undertook the sabotage tasks which had been assigned to them under extremely heavy fire from the 7000 German troops available in the area.

There was considerable fighting in the streets of St Nazaire , 169 soldiers were killed, many taken prisoner , a few succeeded in returning to the UK in the only two launches that got back , and a few succeeded in joining the French Resistance . On the morning of the 28th the Campbeltown was firmly imbedded in the dock gate, the Germans wishing to inspect the damage were a little wary and took several members of the Royal Navy with them on board. Although these men knew the explosives were controlled by a time fuse they said nothing and as a result when the Campeltown blew up, there were several German experts on board and also, unfortunately, some members of the RN.

Tactically , Operation Chariot was a success , the dry dock was out of action for the rest of the war, it was however costly in human lives , including quite a few St Nazaire citizens who fell under the bullets of a completely frenzied German garrison . If your travels take you around that part of world, don't forget to go and pay homage to those buried in this small but very beautiful cemetery.
