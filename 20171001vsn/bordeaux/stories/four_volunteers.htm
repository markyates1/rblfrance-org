‘Les Quatre Volontaires’ - The Jersey Escape Route

Most WW 11 evasion & escape stories are based on British (occasionally American) service personnel, being helped through enemy occupied territory with French civilians, at great risk, providing vital succour and assistance along the way. This is a story of British civilians helping French service personnel and volunteers though enemy occupied territory - Jersey, and across to England. This story begins, and ends, in the small fishing port and seaside resort of Carteret in Normandy. My wife, Jeannine, and I attended a final plaque unveiling in Carteret on June 26th 2004, the logical follow on from a similar ceremony in 1947, a plaque unveiling by the Maire and other important people in order to commemorate in its street names the people who had been in the fore in June 1940 in their non acceptance of the German occupation. Then, every one knew the names of the four volunteers, four local young men, who made their escape from enemy occupied France to Britain, via Jersey.

So it was that in 1947 a street in Carteret was named ‘La rue des Quatre Volontaires’. However, as the years passed, folkloric knowledge no longer applied, so it was recently decided to add another plaque to the street with the names of the four young men who by this time had become just two old men, Clément Millet and myself, Jack Douay, both now in our eighties. The other two ‘Volontaires’, our two comrades, having in the meantime departed for good. Carteret could also boast a much more famous ‘Volontaire’, the future Admiral Thierry d’Argenlieu who, taken prisoner in Cherbourg, had escaped from the lorry taking him to the prison camp and walked 25 Km to Carteret where he knew he could find some friendly help. The fisherman Emile Valmy, who at great risk had taken him, and three of us, to Jersey 30 km away, was of course also honoured. My personal escape story is uneventful and therefore rather dull, but that of my three companions is really worth telling, so I’ll; start with the dull part: mine . My father drove me and my bicycle to Avranches on the 16th June 1940. Avranches is near the Mont St Michel bay; the idea was to get me out of the vicinity of Cherbourg which was obviously going to be a German objective, and enable me to move into a less front line situation where my age of seventeen and a half was less likely to attract the attention of the Germans. On the 17th June the French capitulated, the Germans were already well south of Avranches, there seemed no point for me to continue in that direction so I cycled west on secondary roads and finally ran into the Germans in Dinan. They were still not very well organised, busy moving forward towards Brest and only lightly occupying the conquered territory I was therefore able to continue on the 18th with a minimum amount of German controls to the north coast of Brittany at Val André where I had some friends. I quickly found out that a Boulogne fishing boat in the harbour was on its way back north, a Foreign Legion Officer was trying, without great success, to recruit some very demoralised soldiers to join him in rallying to the UK. After much discussion on account of my age and civilian status he finally agreed to take me with him. This meant discreetly getting aboard the boat during the night without being spotted by any wandering German, and hiding among the smelly fishing gear until the boat sailed . We finally left on the 19th at 5 am in heavy seas that had made the fisherman hesitate at length as to whether he was going to sail or not. This of course added considerably to the risk of being discovered by the Germans, especially as all the discussions took place with the noisy diesel engine warming up ! We arrived in St Helier in Jersey after a 12 hour journey, to find evacuation in full swing and thanks to my British birth certificate which I had hesitantly (on account of the German controls ) kept by me, I was able to leave immediately on a small tramp ship which arrived safely next day in Weymouth.. We had an uneventful crossing, of which I remember very little as I slept happily, for the first time since my 80 Km cycle ride, in the cement dust of the ship’s hold. I obviously felt we were well protected, as we had a couple of soldiers and a rather antique machine gun on deck to defend us against potential air attacks. On arrival I joined some members of my family who very kindly offered me a home during my four years in the UK. I was soon occupied with a job in the drawing office of one of the Rolls Royce aero-engine factories in Scotland, followed by a spell on a large farming estate near Oxford where my health recovered from a Scottish winter coupled with the effects of a 70 hour working week. I was then able to enlist in the RAF, after having done my flying training at schools in South Africa and Egypt; I joined a Spitfire squadron in Italy and returned with it to the UK in time to prepare for D. Day. We then carried out the usual fighter squadron duties of bomber escorts to the Ruhr and fighter sweeps here and there. I left the RAF in 1946 and went to read forestry at Oxford.

Meanwhile in Carteret, (where my family wondered during 3 months what had become of me ) Thierry d’Argenlieu had arrived and was taken to Jersey on the 24th June where aided by the French Consul he quickly found passage to the UK to join the Free French Forces . The other three members of the “Four Volunteers” called up in Cherbourg, had discreetly got back into civilian clothes and made their way back home to Carteret where they started looking for an opportunity to leave. They unfortunately missed the journey on the 24th June, but Emile Valmy agreed to run the risk again and take them to Jersey. They crept on board his boat during the night of the 25th June, a risky affair as by this time the Germans had become rather more vigilant than when I had left, and they landed in Jersey on the 26th. Alas, the last evacuation ship had left the previous night and Jersey was to be totally occupied by the Germans a few days later on the 1st July. There was already a certain confusion, and rumours over the state of war in Jersey which made it very difficult for these new arrivals to know exactly what was happening. Thanks to some family contacts the three were hidden and given hospitality and work in a couple of farms digging up potatoes and picking tomatoes. They were also very busy spotting possible means of leaving the island and thus finally found a suitable boat although it had been made un-seaworthy through the removal of some vital parts of the engine. One of the three was a competent mechanic and, having been given access to a garage workshop, he was able to make the missing parts of the engine without of course having any possibility of testing them before departure. They also needed about 130 litres of petrol to reach the UK, this they collected during night expeditions from motorcars requisitioned by the Germans. Their main method was siphoning , however when this wasn’t possible two of them lifted the car sufficiently for the third one to slip a pitchfork with a previously prepared and sharpened upturned prong under the petrol tank upon which the two then dropped the car to pierce the petrol tank. After two months of preparation and hiding their friendly farmer took them and their stock of petrol tins hidden under his potatoes in his pony cart to Rozel port. There they departed silently at night after having cut the moorings of a number of other boats. To their great relief when they finally dared try the engine it started and they were able to sail for 36 hours to the coast off Dartmouth. This had only been possible thanks to the devoted and highly risky help from a good number of Jersey inhabitants. We in the South West of France are more familiar with French families helping British escapees to hide, have safe passage through enemy occupied territory and then to cross into Spain; opportunities for the reverse to happen were obviously scarce but when they happened it is most gratifying to know that the same help was also forthcoming. To return to our three lads, now just off Dartmouth, they then had an engine failure. Not unreasonably, they expected to see someone come to their rescue. This, however failed to happen. Eventually they managed to get their engine going again and, a bit disgruntled with their welcome, sailed into the port. When they mentioned this to the authorities they were told: ‘We could hardly come and get you, you were in the middle of a minefield!’

After our wartime adventures in the RAF, the Free French Air Force and the Navy, the four of us returned safely to Carteret after the war and were present at the 1947 ceremonies. Clément Milet and André Courval served in several Free French Bomber Squadrons as Flight Engineers and saw considerable action in the Middle East and Western Desert before returning to the UK to continue operations in Europe. Henri Letourneur joined the Free French Navy and took part in many of the convoy escorts in northern oceans between the USA and the U.S.S.R. Unfortunately, only Clément Milet now 83 and myself made it until the 2004 plaque edition ceremony; but together with their families, our thoughts were constantly with the two absentee members of “Les Quatre Volontaires” whom we all missed enormously, in the final commemoration of our youthful refusal to accept defeat in those dark days of 1940.

Jack Douay Bordeaux 7th November 2004
